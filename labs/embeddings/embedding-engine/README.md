Why do embeddings capture meaning?Embeddings capture meaning because they follow the Distributional Hypothesis in linguistics: "Words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings." [1]Contextual Association: During training, a model adjusts a word's vector based on the words surrounding it. For example, if "apple" and "orange" both frequently appear next to "juice", "eat", and "fruit", their vectors are dragged toward the same region in space.Multi-Dimensional Properties: Instead of a single definition, an embedding breaks a concept down into hundreds of continuous numerical coordinates (dimensions). Each dimension implicitly learns to track abstract semantic traits (e.g., is_software, is_liquid, grammatical_tense).Why doesn't one-hot encoding scale?One-hot encoding represents every word as a massive, sparse binary array where only a single index is 1 and all others are 0. This approaches breaks down rapidly because:Memory Explosion (Dimension Bloat): The size of every single vector equals the total size of your vocabulary (\(V\)). If you have a real-world vocabulary of 100,000 words, every single word requires a 100,000-dimensional array. This wastes massive amounts of GPU memory on zeros.Semantic Blindness: In a one-hot space, every single vector is perfectly perpendicular (orthogonal) to every other vector. The dot product between "cat" and "kitten" is exactly 0, just like "cat" and "kubernetes". There is zero geometric capability to measure similarity or distance.Why is embedding dimension important?The embedding dimension (the length of the feature vector, typically 64, 384, 768, or 1536) dictates the capacity and efficiency of your model:If it is too low (e.g., 8 dimensions): The model suffers from underfitting. It does not have enough mathematical space to separate distinct concepts, forcing unrelated topics to overlap and compress together.If it is too high (e.g., 4096 dimensions): The model suffers from overfitting and extreme resource waste. The training data becomes sparse in such a high-dimensional space, requiring massive datasets to train properly, while inflating compute cost and storage bottlenecks.Why are similar words close together?Similar words are grouped closely together because gradient descent explicitly forces them there during the optimization phase.Loss Function Direction: Neural network training loops use objective functions (like Cosine Similarity Loss or Contrastive Loss) that penalize the model if related words are far apart.Weight Adjustments: If the network struggles to predict a blank word in "I ate a juicy [blank]", the backpropagation step modifies the weight matrix rows for both apple and orange in the exact same direction, minimizing spatial distance between them.Why does averaging embeddings lose information?Averaging word vectors (Mean Pooling) to create a sentence embedding removes structural context due to basic arithmetic aggregation:Loss of Word Order (Bag-of-Words Effect): Averaging is a commutative operation. The sentence "The bank blocked the fraud" and "Fraud blocked the bank" result in the exact same average vector, even though the actor and subject are reversed.Semantic Cancellation: If a sentence contains words with radically opposing meanings, adding their positive and negative coordinate weights together cancels them out. This dilutes the vector toward a generic, muddy midpoint that loses the sharp contextual features of individual words.
