1. Why are tensors preferred over NumPy arrays in deep learning?Tensors are preferred over NumPy arrays for three primary reasons:GPU/TPU Acceleration: NumPy arrays are strictly bound to the CPU. PyTorch tensors can be natively moved to accelerators (like Apple Silicon mps, NVIDIA cuda, or Google TPUs), speeding up matrix multiplications by over \(10\times\) to \(100\times\).Automatic Differentiation: PyTorch tensors keep track of the operations performed on them through a computational graph. This tracking allows the library to compute gradients automatically, a feature NumPy lacks.Deep Integration: Tensors integrate natively with PyTorch's deep learning ecosystems, such as memory-efficient data loaders and neural network modules (nn.Module).2. What does backward() actually compute?When you call loss.backward(), PyTorch computes the partial derivatives of the loss with respect to every single trainable parameter (weights and biases) in the network.Mathematically, if your loss is \(L\) and a weight matrix is \(W\), it computes \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}\) using the Chain Rule of calculus.It starts at the loss function and walks backward through your network architecture.The resulting derivative values (gradients) are calculated and stored directly inside each parameter's .grad attribute (e.g., model.linear1.weight.grad).3. Why must gradients be cleared every iteration?PyTorch does not overwrite gradients during the backward pass; it adds (accumulates) them.If you do not call optimizer.zero_grad(), the gradients from your current step will be added to the gradients from your previous steps.This accumulation causes the gradient values to grow massively over time, sending your optimizer in completely wrong directions and breaking the training process.Note: Gradient accumulation is only left intentional when trying to train huge batch sizes that do not fit into GPU memory all at once.4. What happens if the learning rate is extremely high?Overshooting: The optimizer takes steps that are too large, jumping completely over the local minimum (the optimal weight configuration).Divergence: Instead of the loss decreasing, the loss values will rapidly grow, fluctuate wildly, or destabilize.Exploding Gradients / NaNs: The weights can blow up toward infinity, causing your loss and weights to read as NaN (Not a Number) in your terminal.5. What happens if the learning rate is extremely low?Painfully Slow Convergence: The network updates its weights by microscopic amounts, meaning training will take a massive amount of time or epochs to reach a good result.Getting Stuck: The optimizer is highly likely to get permanently stuck in poor local minima or flat regions (saddle points) of the loss landscape, never learning the actual patterns in your dataset.
