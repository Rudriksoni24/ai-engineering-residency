Why Special Tokens ExistSpecial tokens act as metadata markers that tell a neural network how to handle text structure, boundaries, and alignment during training and generation.

<PAD> (Padding Token)The Problem: Neural networks require data to be passed in fixed-size rectangular matrices (batches). However, real-world sentences have completely different lengths.The Purpose: It acts as a meaningless placeholder appended to shorter sentences to pad them out to match the length of the longest sentence in the batch.Model Action: The network uses an attention mask to explicitly ignore these tokens so they do not influence the gradients or final predictions.

<UNK> (Unknown Token)The Problem: A vocabulary cannot contain every single word in existence (e.g., rare names, typos, or new slang).The Purpose: It acts as a catch-all safety net for any out-of-vocabulary word encountered during text processing.Model Action: Instead of crashing or ignoring the word entirely, the tokenizer substitutes the missing word with <UNK>, allowing the network to maintain the structural rhythm of the sentence.

<BOS> / <s> (Beginning of Sequence Token)The Problem: During text generation, an autoregressive model predicts the next word based on previous words. For the very first word, there is zero historical context to read from.The Purpose: It acts as a standardized starting signal or "trigger" that tells the model to begin generating text out of nothing.Model Action: It sets up the internal states of the model to prepare for structured decoding.

<EOS> / </s> (End of Sequence Token)The Problem: Without an explicit stopping signal, a text-generating model would loop infinitely, spitting out words forever until it runs out of memory.The Purpose: It marks the definitive end of a sentence, paragraph, or document structure.Model Action: During training, the model learns when a thought is complete. During inference, as soon as the model predicts the <EOS> token as its next word, the system automatically halts generation.



Why don't GPT models tokenize by words?Word-level tokenization fails in production for three primary reasons:Out-of-Vocabulary (OOV) Exploded Errors: New words, slang, typos, or rare names (like product IDs) cannot fit into a pre-defined word list. They are all permanently lost to the <UNK> token, blinding the model to crucial details.Vocabulary Size Explosion: Tracking every single variant of a word across tenses, singular/plural forms, and prefixes (e.g., run, running, runs, re-run) would require a vocabulary of millions of words, destroying GPU memory capacity.Morphology & Compounding: Morphologically rich languages (like German or Turkish) create massively long compound words. Word-level tokenization cannot break these down into their shared semantic sub-components.Modern GPT models use Sub-word Tokenization (such as Byte-Pair Encoding or BPE) to split rare or long words into tiny, reusable character fragments, completely eliminating the <UNK> token.What problems do emojis create?Emojis disrupt traditional text processing pipelines due to their underlying character structures:Multi-Byte Complexity: Standard text characters take up 1 to 2 bytes in UTF-8 encoding. Many emojis require 4 bytes or more. If a tokenizer splits text by bytes incorrectly, it slices the emoji in half, creating corrupted, unreadable character noise.Zero-Width Joiner (ZWJ) Sequences: Complex emojis (like family groups 👨‍👩‍👧‍👦 or professions) are actually multiple individual emojis glued together by a hidden formatting character called a Zero-Width Joiner (\u200D). If a tokenizer treats these as separate elements, a single cohesive emoji gets broken down into random sub-icons.Skin Tone Modifiers: Emojis with specific skin tones use a base yellow emoji followed immediately by a Fitzpatrick scale modifier token. Bad tokenizers split the modifier from the base, treating skin tones as separate characters instead of unified visual elements.Why are spaces important in tokenization?Spaces are not just visual separations; they are critical semantic anchors for language models:The "Space-Before" Dependency: In modern tokenizers (like GPT-2/GPT-4's tiktoken), the space preceding a word belongs directly to that token (e.g., " banking" and "banking" are treated as two entirely distinct token IDs). This allows the model to differentiate between a word starting a sentence and a word embedded inside a phrase.Context Preservation: Missing or mismanaged spaces can fuse independent concepts together into arbitrary sub-words. This fundamentally alters the embedding vectors and confuses the attention layers during text generation.Whitespace Tracking: Programming languages (like Python) rely on strict whitespace indentation for block syntax structure. The tokenizer must treat consecutive spaces, tabs, and newlines as precise functional units rather than empty, ignorable data.Why is vocabulary size a tradeoff?Choosing the size of a model's vocabulary (\(V\)) requires balancing computational efficiency against sequence throughput:Small Vocabulary Size (e.g., 10,000 tokens)Large Vocabulary Size (e.g., 100,000+ tokens)Pros: Saves massive GPU memory because the final linear layer (prediction head) and embedding matrices remain very small.Pros: Compresses long phrases into single tokens. This reduces the final sequence length, allowing the model to process long documents much faster.Cons: Sentences are fragmented into dozens of tiny sub-tokens, ballooning the sequence length. This consumes context window space rapidly.Cons: The model's weights expand massively. Millions of parameters are wasted on rare, specific words that the model rarely updates during training
